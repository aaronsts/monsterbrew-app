No homebrew monster survives contact with a party unchanged, and that's fine. The published books were playtested for years and still produced the demilich; your creature gets one evening. This chapter covers the checks worth doing before the session, and the adjustments worth making during and after it.

## Pre-flight math checks

Five minutes against the [benchmark table](/guide/challenge-rating) catches most problems before they reach the table:

1. **Row check.** Put the creature's AC, HP, attack bonus, DC, and damage per round next to its CR row. One or two deliberate deviations that express its role: good. Everything high or everything low: relabel the CR.
2. **Offense/defense split.** Estimate the CR row its defenses resemble and the row its offense resembles, and average them. If the average disagrees with the printed CR by more than a point, adjust.
3. **Nova check.** Compute the creature's best possible round (everything hits, recharge available). If that round can take the party's squishiest member from full to zero, decide *on purpose* whether you want that. If you keep it, make sure it's [telegraphed](/guide/traits-actions-flavor).
4. **Worst-case round for the monster.** Now assume it's stunned, restrained, or banished round one. Does it still get to do anything this fight? If not, back to [action economy](/guide/common-pitfalls).
5. **Comparison smell test.** Teos Abadía's favorite check is also the oldest one: put your statblock next to a published monster of the same CR (the [SRD library](/library?source=srd) is right here) and ask which one you'd rather run.

## Gauge the encounter, not just the monster

A perfectly balanced monster in the wrong quantity is still a TPK. The Lazy GM's Resource Document offers a benchmark simple enough to do in your head (CC-BY 4.0):

> An encounter might be deadly if the sum of monster CRs is greater than **¼ of the sum of character levels** (for levels 1–4) or **½ of the sum of character levels** (for level 5+).

Five 4th-level characters (total 20, ÷4 = 5) against four CR 2 ogres (total 8): potentially deadly. The same party at 5th level (total 25, ÷2 = 12): a manageable fight. "Deadly" here means characters dropping and a real chance someone dies. The benchmark is a tachometer redline, not a difficulty dial, and circumstances (terrain, rest state, waves, surprise) push fights across it in both directions. There's also a solo-monster corollary: a single creature might be deadly when its CR meets or exceeds the average party level (or 1.5× it, at 5th level and up).

Character capability jumps at 5th level (extra attacks, *fireball*, Stunning Strike) and again at 11th. Expect the same monster to play very differently on either side of those breaks, and re-run the benchmark rather than trusting last tier's instincts.

## Adjust live, quietly

Monster statistics are yours, including mid-fight. The Lazy GM's "lazy tricks" are the standard toolkit:

- Use **average damage** instead of rolling, and a **static initiative** (12, or 10 + Dex) to speed the table up.
- **Trim hit points on the fly** if the fight has made its point and is now dragging; let the creature drop, flee, or surrender when the story is done. (Within the benchmark's HP *range*, this isn't even cheating.)
- If the party is coasting, add an attack or a few points of damage per hit, or bring in a wave, rather than doubling HP mid-fight.
- Let morale be real: foes rout, bargain, and yield. Fights that end at zero HP every time train players to see monsters as HP bars.

## After the first fight

While it's fresh, ask three questions. Did the creature's *concept* land, with the players describing it the way you imagined it? Did anyone make an interesting decision because of it? Was any round a non-event? Then edit the statblock the same evening: cut the trait nobody noticed, retune the number that felt wrong, note what you changed live so it's official next time.

Your creatures live in [your library](/my-creatures), so iteration is cheap: duplicate the creature, tweak the copy, and keep the version that played better. A monster is done when it runs itself and the table remembers it, not when the math is pretty.
